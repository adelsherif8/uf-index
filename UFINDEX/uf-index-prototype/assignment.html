Who coaches whom

A hundred assessments arrive and there are five coaches. This is how they get divided, why the obvious answer is the wrong one, and the five rules still to settle.

The fact that decides it

UFAS does not take clients one at a time. They arrive as cohorts — Gurgaon Police, PPS, IOU, Everon — thirty people from one station who know each other, share a shift pattern, and compare notes. Every sensible-sounding way of splitting them evenly destroys that, and it is the thing worth protecting.

01

The four ways to split

All four are built. The choice is one argument to a function, so it can be changed later — but not retroactively, because clients will already be with coaches.

Keep each organisation together

organization  ·  RECOMMENDED

Everyone from one organisation goes to the coach who already has people from it. That coach learns how a police shift rota works once, instead of five coaches each learning it badly.

Costs: caseloads end up uneven — 25 against 16 — and a cohort bigger than one coach's cap has to be split anyway.

Give to whoever has fewest clients

lowest_load  ·  the default and the fallback

Fewest active clients wins; ties break on who has been waiting longest. Perfectly even, and completely blind to who the person is.

Costs: shreds every cohort. Thirty colleagues from one station end up with five different coaches and five different answers.

Strict rotation

round_robin

Next client goes to whoever went longest without one. Trivial to explain, which is its whole appeal — nobody can claim favouritism.

Costs: the same cohort damage as lowest-load, plus it ignores how heavy each caseload actually is. Ten quiet clients and ten in crisis count the same.

Match their weakest area to a specialism

specialism

Whichever of energy, sleep or body-feeling is lowest on their last check-in picks the coach who works in it.

Costs: only worth anything if coaches genuinely differ. Body composition is excluded on purpose — it sets the score, so it is lowest for nearly everyone and would send the whole intake to one coach.

02

The same 100 clients, four ways

Six real cohorts — 34, 22, 16, 12, 9, 7 — five coaches, a cap of 25 each. The last column is the one that matters.

StrategyCaseloadsCohorts split apart
Keep organisations together 25 · 22 · 19 · 18 · 16 1 of 6
Give to whoever has fewest 20 · 20 · 20 · 20 · 20 6 of 6
Strict rotation 20 · 20 · 20 · 20 · 20 6 of 6
Match specialism depends on the intake 6 of 6

Perfectly even caseloads cost you every cohort. Organisation-first keeps five of six whole, and the one it splits — Gurgaon Police at 34 — only splits because 34 does not fit in a cap of 25. The evenness the other three buy is worth less than the context they spend.

03

Five rules still to settle

The strategy is the easy half. These are the decisions that actually shape the work, and none of them are mine to make.

1 · What is a real caseload?

The cap is currently 25, which is a number I invented. It is a hard stop: a coach at capacity is skipped, and if every coach is full the assignment refuses rather than quietly overloading someone.

Ask Parina and Manjula where coaching turns into admin. Their answer is the number.

2 · When does someone get a coach?

  • On sign-up — everyone has a coach at once, including people who never take a check-in. Caseloads fill with ghosts.
  • On first check-in — only people who actually engaged get assigned.
  • Manually — you work the queue yourself. Most control, stops scaling around fifty.

On first check-in. A coach's caseload should be people, not sign-ups.

3 · What happens when a cohort outgrows one coach?

Thirty-four Gurgaon Police clients do not fit in a caseload of 25.

  • Split it — 25 to the first coach, 9 to the next. Two coaches now hold one station.
  • Move the whole thing to a coach with room. Keeps it whole, needs someone free.
  • Raise the cap for that coach — a deliberate exception, recorded.

Split, but always to the same second coach, so a station never has more than two. Splitting is what the cap is for.

4 · Can a client ask for a particular coach?

Not built, and this matters more than the strategy does. Some clients will want a female coach, or someone who speaks their language. If a request like that arrives after assignment it becomes a transfer, which costs the client the relationship they had started.

Decide this before switching automatic assignment on. If clients can ask, the strategy becomes a default rather than a rule — and that is a different build.

5 · Does anything rebalance on its own?

If Parina ends on 30 and Manjula on 5, does the system move people?

No. Moving a client mid-programme costs them their coach and gains you a tidier number. Surface it on the Coaches screen and let a person decide.

What is already built

BehaviourHow it works
Every assignment records why Each row stores the strategy that produced it, so a bad bulk run can be found and reversed instead of guessed at.
Capacity is a hard stop A full coach is skipped. If everyone is full it raises an error rather than putting someone in a queue nobody watches.
Every strategy falls back No organisation history, no matching specialism, nobody with room — it drops to lowest-load rather than leaving a person unassigned.
Transfers keep the history Moving a client ends the old assignment and starts a new one, so who held them in July stays answerable. Their unstarted sessions with the old coach are cancelled.
Assignment is idempotent A client who already has a coach keeps them. Re-running the bulk assign is safe.
Admins are never assigned Only role = 'coach' can receive clients.

In the dashboard: All clients → Assign everyone waiting for a bulk run, or Move on any client to reassign one. Both are admin-only.
